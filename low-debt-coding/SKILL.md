## Objective

Reduce unnecessary token consumption across the current task and long-term maintenance, without sacrificing implementation quality. Avoid irrelevant investigation, unsupported compatibility obligations, speculative defenses, and duplicated capabilities. Reduce technical debt that repeatedly increases the context, reasoning, changes, and verification required for later tasks.

Choose clear, reliable, maintainable implementations. Do not optimize for the fewest changed lines, the least immediate effort, or merely passing the supplied example. Prefer materially better implementations when concrete benefits justify the additional work. Do not use hypothetical future savings to justify unrelated refactoring or speculative design.

## 1. Project Technology Baseline

- **New projects:** Choose the latest mutually compatible stable versions of the language, runtime, framework, and main tools. Use modern practices and native capabilities of that baseline rather than legacy patterns copied from older environments. Verify version-sensitive choices that affect implementation without turning selection into an unrelated technology survey.
- **Existing projects:** Build on the project's chosen stack. Prefer clear, mature, modern practices and native features supported by its current versions. Follow historical conventions only when they still serve the project. After a stable release, retain the chosen language version and runtime/framework major versions until an upgrade is explicitly approved.
- **Version maintenance:** When dependency maintenance is requested or required by the task, apply compatible security fixes and maintenance updates according to project policy. Do not turn routine feature work into a dependency audit or upgrade exercise. Treat major upgrades as deliberate technical decisions or separate tasks. Complete routine feature development, bug fixes, and refactoring on the current baseline by default.
- **Compatibility:** Honor explicit requirements and current project support commitments. Do not invent support for older hardware, operating systems, browsers, runtimes, or dependencies. Historical code and documentation alone do not establish a current compatibility obligation. Retain migration, initialization, and data-integrity logic required for correct operation.
- **Scope and conventions:** Follow valid project tools and conventions without reproducing flawed practices. Address debt that blocks a correct solution, would be extended by the change, or belongs to an implementation directly replaced by the task. Leave unrelated issues outside the task. Mention them only when they materially affect the user's decisions or reveal a significant concrete risk; do not investigate them further by default.

## 2. Investigate Before Implementing

- **Decision-driven investigation:** Read the implementation, callers, contracts, tests, configuration, lockfiles, CI, or deployment information needed for the current decision. These are evidence sources, not a mandatory checklist. Do not infer support requirements from local machine versions or file extensions. Expand investigation for concrete dependencies, contradictions, failures, or material uncertainties. Proceed when the evidence supports a correct implementation and important design choices; stop investigating questions unlikely to change those judgments.
- **Resolve consequential uncertainties:** Verify version conflicts, support status, and deprecations against official documentation for the applicable versions when they affect the solution. Ask the user if a correctness-critical question remains unresolved after focused investigation; continue independent work in the meantime.
- **Documentation freshness:** Treat comments and documentation as evidence of intent, not guaranteed descriptions of current behavior. Resolve task-relevant conflicts with code, tests, configuration, and explicit requirements before relying on them. Implementation establishes what the system does; current requirements and valid contracts establish what it should do. Neither old prose nor existing code is automatically correct. Do not restore removed behavior merely because stale documentation describes it. Consult focused history only when needed to resolve a consequential ambiguity.
- **Discover before adding:** Before introducing a component, utility, feature, or business rule, search for equivalent or related capabilities in the project and its existing frameworks and dependencies. Search by responsibility and business concept, not only by a proposed symbol name. Inspect relevant implementations and representative callers. Missing conversation context is not evidence that a capability does not exist. Keep the search focused on likely locations and relevant existing APIs. Once a suitable implementation is found and its contract is understood, use it; do not survey alternatives without a concrete reason.
- **Reuse deliberately:** Reuse implementations that meet the required contract and quality needs. For small differences, prefer a focused extension, improvement, or extraction of genuinely shared behavior over copying code. Do not create parallel components or utilities because starting fresh is easier than understanding existing code. Keep separate implementations when responsibilities or contracts materially differ or reuse would introduce excessive coupling; superficial similarity does not justify merging independently evolving business rules.

## 3. Business Rule Ownership

- **Avoid excessive defensive programming:** Validate at actual trust boundaries and handle realistic failures relevant to the supported system. Inside established contracts, rely on their guarantees. Do not repeat upstream validation, add null checks for guaranteed values, provide fallbacks for unsupported environments, retry without a recovery rationale, or catch exceptions merely to hide failure. Fix broken contracts at the responsible boundary or implementation instead of spreading compensating checks across callers.
- **Single source of truth:** Adding more data of the same kind should not require duplicating formulas, conditional branches, or structures. Reuse should reduce duplicated rules and places that must change together, rather than add wrappers, interfaces, or files without practical value. Keep rules that evolve independently separate.
- **Responsibility boundaries:** Organize responsibilities around current needs to prevent duplicated logic from spreading. Local changes to a rule should remain within its responsible module and related tests. When small changes repeatedly touch unrelated modules or depend on hidden global knowledge, investigate scattered rules and coupling.

## 4. Choose Proportionate Implementations

- **Abstractions and configurability:** Use abstractions to clarify existing responsibility boundaries, centralize shared rules, or isolate changes and tests. An interface with a single implementation can serve these purposes; a general framework for a single reuse case is usually unnecessary. Prefer capabilities provided by the language and framework over mechanically adding layers or wrappers to follow design patterns.
- **Dependencies:** Start by checking the standard library and existing dependencies. Evaluate new dependencies based on actual behavior, version compatibility, maintenance, and benefits. Prefer native capabilities when the current versions can replace workarounds for older technology baselines. Within the current task's scope, remove dependencies, wrappers, or complex workarounds whose sole purpose is to compensate for features missing from older versions. Avoid high-maintenance dependencies for minor conveniences; use mature implementations for complex security, parsing, and protocol functionality.
- **Complete replacements and consolidation:** When replacing or extracting shared functionality, update affected in-scope callers, configuration, and tests together. Remove superseded implementations, aliases, compatibility wrappers, fallback branches, and parallel paths unless current contracts still require them. Do not introduce a shared implementation while leaving redundant versions in the affected paths. Check callers, registration points, and configuration before deletion; retain necessary operational, initialization, migration, and data-integrity logic. Keep unrelated consolidation and toolchain upgrades outside the task.
- **Maintain documentation:** Update or remove task-relevant comments and documentation made inaccurate by the change or confirmed to describe superseded behavior. Remove obsolete instructions, compatibility claims, and descriptions of deleted paths rather than leaving contradictory accounts. Explain current constraints instead of restating code; make TODOs specific and actionable. Do not expand this into an unrelated documentation audit.
- **Version control and recovery:** Rely on the project's VCS for source-code history and recovery. Do not create precautionary backup copies, `.bak` files, parallel directories, or commented-out old implementations. Do not make unsolicited commits or stashes, or overwrite unrelated uncommitted work. For requested rollbacks, restore only the relevant changes. For databases, datasets, persistent storage, or external-service state that VCS cannot restore, assess recovery needs and apply safeguards proportionate to the actual risk before destructive operations.

## 5. Verification, Reporting, and Completion

- **Verify proportionately:** Use the project's existing formatting, type-checking, build, and testing tools. Start with checks relevant to the change and checks required by the project; expand their scope when failures or unresolved risks warrant it.
- **Test meaningful behavior:** For low-impact, easily reversible changes, tests that simply mirror implementation logic offer little value. When testing is warranted, cover representative input classes, boundaries, bug reproductions, and important invariants beyond the supplied examples. When scale matters, check behavior as data grows and use parameterized or property-based tests where appropriate. Do not mechanically add checks once the relevant behavior and risks are adequately covered.
- **Report actual completion:** Briefly state the substantive changes, checks actually performed and their results, and unresolved issues affecting correctness or use. Distinguish unverified behavior from failed verification. Do not present implementation completion as proof that verification passed.
